Module 1 overview

Most beginners arrive at lead brokering with a fuzzy idea — something like "I find people who want a service, then I sell those people to businesses that provide the service." That sentence is roughly right, but it papers over enough distinctions to cause real problems later: pricing distinctions that double or halve revenue, legal distinctions that determine which laws apply to which steps of your operation, and operational distinctions that determine what kind of business you actually run.

Module 1 is the vocabulary and structural-economics module. It is the foundation everything else in the course rests on. The goal is not to make you an expert in any single vertical or regulation — those are Modules 2 and 3. The goal is to make sure that when you read a trade publication, listen to an industry podcast, or sit in a meeting with a prospective lead buyer, you understand what is being said.

The module is five lessons:

  • 1.1 Definitions, plainly stated.
  • 1.2 The four players.
  • 1.3 Business models.
  • 1.4 Brief industry history.
  • 1.5 Why the industry exists.

Three deliverables ship with this module: the industry glossary (PDF), the money-flow diagram (PNG/SVG), and the 20 major industry players reference (PDF).

Lesson 1.1 — Definitions, plainly stated

Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you can distinguish between a contact, a lead, a qualified lead, a prospect, and a customer; and between the activities of lead generation, lead brokering, lead aggregation, pay-per-call, and lead resale. You can also explain, in one sentence each, why these distinctions matter for pricing and legal exposure.

Why the words matter

Industry terminology in lead brokering is loose. The same word — "lead" — describes radically different things in different conversations. A lead from a Facebook click-through that captured only an email and a zip code is not the same asset as a recorded inbound phone call from someone who already named the service they wanted. They are priced differently, contracted for differently, and regulated differently.

Pretending the differences don't exist is the single most common cause of preventable disasters in this industry: a broker quotes a buyer using one definition, delivers using another, and the contract dispute that follows is the kind of expensive lesson the course is designed to help you skip.

This lesson defines the terms operators actually use. Where you'll see two reasonable definitions in circulation, the course notes which one is more common and uses that one consistently for the rest of the curriculum.

The five things people call leads

1. Contact

A piece of consumer data — name, email, phone number — with no expressed interest in any particular product or service. Contacts are the raw material of cold outreach, not lead generation. Buying lists of "contacts" and treating them as leads is the source of an enormous amount of TCPA and CAN-SPAM exposure.

Why this matters: if someone offers to sell you 100,000 "leads" for $500, you are almost certainly looking at contacts — usually scraped or repurposed from elsewhere. The price tells you what the asset really is. Real leads, in any vertical, do not trade at $0.005 each.

2. Lead

A specific person who has, on a specific date, expressed interest in a specific service or product, by performing an affirmative act: submitting a form, calling a number, opting in to a list, clicking through a quote-comparison flow. The affirmative act, the timestamp, and the consent record are the three components that make a lead a lead in the industry's working definition.

The minimum data set most buyers will accept: name, phone, email, zip code, the service requested, the date and time of submission, and (increasingly) a TrustedForm or Jornaya certificate proving the consent was captured cleanly. Anything less is a partial lead; some buyers accept partials at a discount, most do not.

3. Qualified lead

A lead that meets specific criteria the buyer has defined contractually. The criteria vary by vertical. In personal injury, qualification typically means the consumer was injured within a defined recent window, the injury required medical treatment, and the consumer has not yet retained counsel. In roofing, qualification typically means the consumer owns the home, the roof is older than a defined threshold, and the property is in the buyer's service area. In Medicare, qualification means age 65 or older (or qualifying disability), Medicare-eligible, and looking to enroll or switch plans during a valid election period.

"Qualified" is a contractual term, not an objective one. Whatever the lead purchase agreement says qualified means is what qualified means for purposes of returns, credits, and disputes.

4. Prospect

A lead that the buyer has begun working — meaning initial contact has been made, qualification questions have been asked, and the consumer is in the buyer's sales pipeline. Prospects are not delivered; they are created by the buyer's intake team out of leads. The distinction matters because the broker is rarely paid based on prospect-stage outcomes; payment is almost always tied to lead delivery or, in pay-per-call models, to a qualified call.

5. Customer

A prospect that has transacted — signed a retainer, scheduled the install, bought the policy, closed on the loan. Customers belong entirely to the buyer; in almost no compliant contract does the broker have any continuing relationship with, or claim on, the consumer after the lead has been delivered.

Why this hierarchy matters: pricing climbs as you move down the list. A contact is worth pennies. A lead is worth dollars. A qualified lead is worth tens to hundreds of dollars. A prospect is worth more than the lead that produced it (the buyer's intake has added value). A customer is worth whatever the buyer's lifetime gross margin on that customer is — often four to five orders of magnitude more than the contact at the top of the funnel.

The five activities that get called "lead generation"

Just as "lead" is overloaded, so is "lead generation." In the industry, five distinct activities live under that umbrella term, and the differences determine what kind of business you'd actually be running, what compliance burden you'd carry, and what your unit economics look like.

Lead generation (narrow definition)

The original creation of leads through marketing activity that you yourself run: paid search, paid social, SEO, content, email, organic social, partnerships. You are the consumer's first compliant point of contact. You wrote the privacy policy they checked, you control the disclosure language, and the TrustedForm certificate is captured on your domain.

Operational profile: a media-buying or SEO operation. The biggest costs are ad spend, creative production, and (in the case of paid search/social) the platform learning curve. The biggest skill investment is in conversion optimization and consent compliance.

Lead brokering

Buying leads from generators (or aggregators) and reselling them to advertisers, typically with some normalization, scoring, or routing logic added. Some brokers generate a portion of their inventory themselves and buy the rest — the distinction between "broker" and "generator" is not a clean line. In trade discussions, "broker" usually implies someone who relies meaningfully on inventory from other generators, not just their own.

Operational profile: relationship-driven on both sides. The brokerage's biggest assets are buyer contracts and generator supply relationships. The skill set leans toward sales, contracts, and inventory management.

Lead aggregation

Sitting between many generators and many advertisers, normalizing leads from disparate sources into a single inventory pool, deduplicating, scoring, and routing to buyers based on contracted criteria. Aggregators operate at meaningful scale — they are the marketplaces of the industry. Examples that buyers will name in conversations: QuinStreet, Nationwide, Hometown Quotes (insurance); X Social Media, Legal Brand Marketing (legal-adjacent); Modernize, Networx (home services).

Operational profile: technology- and operations-heavy. Most aggregators run on platforms like LeadConduit (ActiveProspect), Boberdoo, Phonexa, or Leadspedia, or have built their own. The skill set is technical operations, traffic-source diversification, and large-account sales.

Pay-per-call

A monetization model in which the deliverable is a qualified phone call to the buyer, not a form submission. The call must typically last a minimum duration (often 30, 60, or 90 seconds), originate from a defined geography, occur within the buyer's business hours, and not duplicate a recent call. Networks like Ringba and Retreaver are the common platforms; the largest pay-per-call advertisers are in insurance (Medicare, ACA, auto), home services, and legal.

Operational profile: call-center-adjacent. A pay-per-call operation often runs paid media to a number, not a form, and uses live agents or IVR (interactive voice response) qualification before connecting the call. Compliance posture is meaningfully different — TCPA exposure, TSR (Telemarketing Sales Rule) obligations, and recording-consent rules all bite harder when phone calls are the deliverable.

Lead resale

The secondary sale of leads — a lead that has already been sold once, being sold again. Sometimes this is a buyer that didn't close the lead reselling it back to an aggregator ("lead returns" that get redistributed). Sometimes it's the same generator multi-selling a non-exclusive lead to additional buyers. Both occur, and both are legitimate when handled in compliance with the underlying consent record. They are also the source of disproportionate legal exposure — every additional seller in a lead's chain is another party the consumer didn't consent to be contacted by, unless the consent record explicitly contemplated downstream resale.

What regulations require: Prior Express Written Consent (PEWC) under the TCPA must be specific to the seller — meaning the entity that will be making the calls or texts. If the consent disclosure named only the original generator, downstream resellers do not have valid consent for autodialed or prerecorded outreach to that consumer. After the 11th Circuit's January 2025 decision in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC, the FCC's stricter "one-to-one" interpretation is no longer in force, but the underlying TCPA standard still requires that consent be valid for the actual seller doing the contacting.

Why the distinctions matter — three concrete consequences

1. Pricing

An exclusive form lead, a shared form lead, and a qualified phone call are not the same product and do not trade at the same prices. Within personal injury, for example, a shared form lead might trade at $50–150, an exclusive form lead at $250–650+, and a signed-case lead (where the generator is paid only after the firm signs the retainer) at $500–2,000+. Confusing these in a buyer conversation will either underprice your inventory (loss of margin) or overprice it (loss of the deal).

2. Legal exposure

TCPA, FTC Endorsement Guides, the FTC Reviews and Testimonials Rule, CAN-SPAM, FCRA, and state privacy laws all have different application surfaces depending on what you're doing. A pure SEO-driven lead generator capturing email-only consent has different obligations than a pay-per-call operator running paid social to a click-to-call number. Module 3 maps the regulatory landscape in detail; for now, the lesson is that the activity definition determines the rule set, so misnaming the activity to yourself can mean misapplying the rules.

3. Operational model

A media-buying operation needs a media buyer, a creative pipeline, a consent capture stack, and an analytics layer. An aggregation operation needs a distribution platform, a buyer-management function, and a supply-management function. A pay-per-call operation needs call infrastructure, IVR/agent design, and a quality scoring loop. These are different businesses with different cost structures and different talent needs. Module 6 goes deep on the operational stack; for now, the point is that the "lead gen" label hides three or four meaningfully different operating models.

On-camera summary (script anchor for this lesson)

The single most useful thing you can do early in this industry is to slow down and use precise language. Five words show up in the same sentence and mean different things: contact, lead, qualified lead, prospect, and customer. They climb a value ladder, from pennies to four-figure-plus assets, and pricing tracks the ladder.

On the activity side, "lead generation" actually covers five distinct things — narrow lead generation, lead brokering, lead aggregation, pay-per-call, and lead resale. Each one is a different business with different compliance surfaces. When operators argue past each other on podcasts, it's almost always because one is talking about narrow generation and the other about brokering or aggregation.

Get the vocabulary right and three problems get easier: pricing your inventory, knowing which laws apply to which step, and choosing what kind of operation you actually want to run.

Lesson 1.2 — The four players

Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you can draw a simple diagram of the four players in the lead brokering ecosystem (advertisers, networks/exchanges, brokers, traffic sources), trace how information moves from consumer to advertiser, and trace how money moves in the opposite direction. You can name at least two real platforms or companies at each layer.

The ecosystem in one paragraph

Strip away the brand names and the platform-specific jargon, and the lead brokering ecosystem has four functional roles. Traffic sources put consumers in front of an offer. Generators and brokers turn the consumer's expressed interest into a structured asset (a lead) and route that asset to a buyer. Networks and exchanges sit in the middle as the plumbing that moves leads from supply side to demand side at scale, with deduplication, scoring, and routing rules. Advertisers — the end buyers — receive the leads and turn them into customers.

Every operator in the industry is doing some combination of these four jobs. The platforms (LeadConduit, Boberdoo, Phonexa, Leadspedia) are the software layer enabling networks. Aggregator brands (QuinStreet, Modernize, Networx) are networks dressed in a consumer-facing brand. Brokers are sales-and-relationship operations sitting in the buy/sell flow. Generators are media operations creating the original lead inventory.

The four players

Player 1: Advertisers (end buyers)

The lawyer, contractor, agent, lender, dentist, or any other service provider who buys leads to close customers. The advertiser is the only player in the chain that actually transacts with the end consumer. Every dollar in the ecosystem ultimately comes from advertisers.

Advertiser profiles vary widely:

  • Solo operators (single-attorney PI firms, single-office medical practices) typically buy 5–50 leads per month, are price-sensitive, and have unsophisticated intake processes.
  • Mid-size firms and franchises (10–50-attorney PI firms, regional roofing chains, multi-location HVAC companies) typically buy 100–1,000 leads per month, have dedicated intake staff, and have clear contractual standards for what they'll accept.
  • Large advertisers (national insurance carriers, large law firms, mass-tort plaintiff firms) buy in the thousands or tens of thousands of leads per month, have professional procurement teams, and demand certified consent records and audited quality reporting.

Operator anecdote: in industry podcasts and conference talks, operators repeatedly note that solo and mid-size advertisers are the entry point for new brokers because the larger advertisers require a track record. The starter relationships are with operators who buy 10–100 leads/month, are willing to work with new vendors, and provide useful early feedback. The course covers this in detail in Module 4.

Player 2: Networks and exchanges

Two related but distinct functions sit in this layer. A network is an operating brand that buys leads from many generators and sells to many advertisers, taking inventory risk and adding value through deduplication, scoring, and routing. An exchange is a more open marketplace where many supply-side and demand-side parties transact, sometimes through a software-only platform with minimal editorial control over inventory.

The platforms that power networks and exchanges:

  • LeadConduit (ActiveProspect): the dominant lead distribution platform. Used by aggregators, large generators, and many brokers to receive, filter, enhance, and route leads.
  • Boberdoo: long-running lead distribution platform popular with mid-size networks across insurance, home services, and legal.
  • Phonexa: an end-to-end performance marketing platform with strengths in call routing, attribution, and CRM.
  • Leadspedia: lead distribution and CRM for affiliate networks.
  • CAKE (Cake): performance marketing tracking platform, more affiliate-network-flavored than pure lead routing, but used in lead-gen affiliate setups.

Examples of network/exchange brands operators will name:

  • Insurance: QuinStreet (NextGen Leads, Modern Health), MediaAlpha, Hometown Quotes, AllWebLeads, EverQuote, Datalot, SmartFinancial.
  • Home services: Modernize, Networx, HomeAdvisor (now Angi Leads), CraftJack.
  • Legal: Legal Brand Marketing, ngage, 4LegalLeads, X Social Media.
  • Medicare and senior: TZ Insurance Solutions (TogetherHealth), Senior Market Sales (purchase pipeline), MediaAlpha (Medicare vertical).

Note: the deliverable at the end of Module 1 — the 20 Major Industry Players reference — covers each of these in more detail with descriptions, primary verticals, and links.

Player 3: Brokers

Independent operators who source leads (some self-generated, some bought from generators or networks) and sell to advertisers, typically with some specialization — a single vertical, a single sub-vertical, a single geography, or a single buyer profile. Many brokers operate as one or two-person businesses. Some are 10–50-person operations that look more like small agencies.

The economic role of the broker, in plain terms: they bridge a fragmented supply base (many generators with inconsistent quality) and a fragmented demand base (many advertisers with idiosyncratic intake processes), and they get paid the spread. A broker without specialization — without a clear thesis about which leads they're best at supplying to which buyers — is a price-taker. A broker with a defensible specialization is a price-maker within that niche.

Player 4: Traffic sources

The actual origin of consumer interest. The buckets:

  • Paid search (Google Ads, Bing/Microsoft Ads). Intent-based traffic. The dominant traffic source for high-CPL verticals like legal, home services, and B2B.
  • Paid social (Meta, TikTok, LinkedIn, Snapchat). Interruption traffic. Dominant for emotional / impulse verticals like debt relief, certain mass torts, beauty and cosmetic procedures.
  • SEO and organic content. Slowest to build, highest margin once established. Major share of lead generation in mortgage, insurance comparison, and some local services.
  • Email and SMS. Either to owned lists or rented placements. Major in financial services, insurance, and education.
  • Affiliate networks. Independent publishers and media buyers who run traffic to a generator's offer and are paid per converted lead. Major in insurance, finance, and education.
  • Outbound B2B (cold email, LinkedIn outreach, calling). Distinct enough that the operational profile is closer to a B2B agency than to consumer lead gen; covered in Module 5.
  • Co-registration and lead exchange paths (consumer fills out one form and is opted into multiple offers in the same flow). Increasingly disfavored from a compliance perspective; many sophisticated buyers no longer accept co-reg leads.

How information flows — consumer to advertiser

Trace a single lead through the ecosystem. A consumer in Atlanta is rear-ended; that evening, she searches "car accident lawyer Atlanta" on her phone. The first paid result is an ad for a legal lead generator's landing page. She clicks, lands on a multi-step quiz that qualifies her (date of accident, injury status, current treatment, prior representation). She submits the form. At submission, a TrustedForm certificate is captured by the generator — a tamper-evident record of the consent disclosure she saw, including the named parties whose calls and texts she authorized.

In the seconds after submission, the generator's stack runs the lead through validation (phone valid, address valid, not on a DNC list, not a duplicate of recent submissions) and posts it to a lead distribution platform like LeadConduit. From there, routing rules send the lead to a network — say, a personal injury network that sells to mid-size PI firms. The network's filters check whether the lead matches the criteria of any of its active buyer contracts (zip code, injury type, treatment status, exclusivity rules) and routes the lead to the matched buyer (or buyers, in a shared model).

At the advertiser's CRM (or intake spreadsheet, for less sophisticated buyers), the lead lands within seconds of the consumer hitting submit. An intake staffer calls the consumer within the first 5–15 minutes — the conversion window that operators publicly cite as roughly five times more important than any other intake variable. If the consumer answers, a 5–30 minute intake conversation begins.

How money flows — advertiser to traffic source

Money runs in the opposite direction. The advertiser pays the broker (or the network, depending on the path) a per-lead price set in the lead purchase agreement. The broker pays the network or generator a cost-per-lead set in their supply agreement. The generator pays the traffic source — Google, Meta, the affiliate, the SEO contractor — out of ad spend or paid commissions.

Each layer takes a margin. In well-running operations, every layer is providing real value (quality filtering, routing, financial-risk absorption, sales relationships) and the layer is compensated proportionate to that value. In less well-running operations, layers stack without adding value and the buyer-side CPL rises to a level that breaks the advertiser's unit economics — at which point the chain restructures or the advertiser brings generation in-house.

A simplified example (made up, but representative of the kind of math you'll see in real chains):

  • Advertiser pays broker: $200 per exclusive PI lead.
  • Broker pays network: $140 per lead.
  • Network pays generator: $90 per lead.
  • Generator's ad spend: $50 per converted lead.
  • Generator's gross margin: $40 ($90 − $50).
  • Network's gross margin: $50 ($140 − $90).
  • Broker's gross margin: $60 ($200 − $140).

The actual margins vary enormously by vertical and operator. The point is that there is room in the chain for multiple parties when each is adding real value. The point is also that when an advertiser is paying $400 for a lead generated for $50 of ad spend, you should be asking what every layer between them is adding — and whether the chain could be shortened.

Variations and edge cases worth knowing

  • Direct generator-to-advertiser. Some generators sell directly to large advertisers, cutting out the network and broker layers. Common in high-volume verticals like Medicare and auto insurance, where the largest carriers have procurement teams that buy direct.
  • Vertically integrated operations. Some companies do generation, aggregation, and direct sale under one roof. QuinStreet is the canonical example; Hometown Quotes is another. The pros and cons of vertical integration are covered in Module 6.
  • White-label arrangements. A broker or network may operate as the back end for a buyer's branded lead-gen site, with the buyer presenting the site as their own intake page. Common in legal and franchise verticals.
  • Live transfer / warm transfer. Instead of a form lead, the generator's call center qualifies the consumer on the phone, then transfers the live, qualified call to the buyer. Priced significantly higher than form leads; covered in Lesson 1.3 and Module 4.

On-camera summary (script anchor for this lesson)

Four players: advertisers, networks/exchanges, brokers, traffic sources. Information moves consumer-to-advertiser through generators and networks; money moves the opposite way, with each layer taking a margin proportional to the value it adds.

The platforms enabling the middle layer are LeadConduit, Boberdoo, Phonexa, and Leadspedia. The aggregator brands you'll hear named most are vertical-specific: QuinStreet and MediaAlpha in insurance, Modernize and Networx in home services, Legal Brand Marketing and 4LegalLeads in legal, EverQuote and SmartFinancial in auto.

If you remember one thing from this lesson, remember that the chain length matters. Long chains stack margins; short chains compress them. The advertiser's tolerance for chain length is set by what their CAC math will bear.

Lesson 1.3 — Business models

Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you can describe the five primary business models used in lead brokering (per-lead flat fee with its exclusive and shared variants, pay-per-call, pay-per-appointment/CPA, retainer/subscription, and revenue share), name real per-lead price ranges for at least four major verticals as of 2024–2025, and explain why revenue share is rare and legally fraught in legal, insurance, and mortgage.

The five business models

1. Per-lead flat fee

A defined dollar amount paid for each delivered lead that meets contracted specifications. This is by an enormous margin the most common business model in the industry. Two variants matter:

Exclusive: the lead is sold to one buyer, and one only. The buyer is the sole party receiving that consumer's information through this channel. Exclusive pricing is meaningfully higher than shared — typically 2 to 5 times higher in the same vertical — and the exclusivity is what the buyer is paying the premium for. The intake conversion rate on exclusive leads is materially higher because the consumer is not simultaneously being called by three or four other firms.

Shared: the lead is sold to multiple buyers in the same geography, typically two to four, occasionally as many as eight. The buyers compete on speed of contact and intake quality. Shared pricing is lower; intake conversion rates are also meaningfully lower because the consumer is fielding multiple calls in a short window.

Most buyers, in most verticals, will buy a mix — some shared inventory for volume and some exclusive inventory for the higher-converting close opportunities. As a broker, having both products in your pricing card is the norm.

2. Pay-per-call (PPC, but the call kind)

Payment is per qualified inbound phone call to the buyer. The qualification criteria are contracted: the call must originate from a defined geography, occur within the buyer's hours, last a minimum duration (commonly 30, 60, or 90 seconds — sometimes longer), and not be a duplicate of a recent call from the same number.

Pay-per-call rates run higher per unit than form-lead rates in the same vertical because the buyer is receiving a live human on the phone, not a form to chase. In Medicare, a qualified inbound call commonly runs $25–60 during AEP (annual election period); in auto insurance, $15–40; in legal, $50–200; in home services, $25–100 depending on sub-vertical.

Operationally, pay-per-call requires a call routing stack (Ringba and Retreaver are the names that come up most), often live agents or sophisticated IVR to qualify before transfer, and a different compliance posture under the TCPA and FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule. Module 4 and Module 6 cover the operational stack.

3. Pay-per-appointment / cost-per-acquisition (CPA)

Payment is contingent on a downstream event — a booked appointment, a signed retainer, a closed sale. This is a high-trust model: the broker carries all the risk between lead delivery and the qualifying event. CPA-style models exist primarily in legal (the "signed-case" model in personal injury and mass torts) and increasingly in some home services niches ("booked appointment" or "job sold" pricing for solar, roofing storm cases, and HVAC replacement).

Operator note: CPA pricing per signed case can run very high — $500–2,000+ for a signed personal injury retainer is normal, $1,500–5,000+ for mass tort retainers in active litigation. The catch, as operators publicly discuss, is that the broker only gets paid for the cases the buyer's intake signs. A buyer with weak intake will turn cheap signed cases into expensive CPA charges, and a buyer with strong intake will make the broker's CPA work look like a steal. This is why CPA pricing is almost always negotiated alongside specific intake protocols.

4. Retainer / subscription

A monthly fee, sometimes plus a per-lead fee at lower per-unit cost, in exchange for a guaranteed lead volume or service level. Closer in feel to an agency engagement than to a pure broker relationship. Common in B2B verticals (where buying signal is sparser and lead-by-lead pricing is awkward), and in some local-services and franchise relationships where the buyer wants predictability.

Retainers reduce the buyer's variance and shift cash-flow risk to the broker — the broker gets paid whether or not they deliver the contracted volume that month, and is contractually on the hook to deliver it. For new operators, retainers are usually a bad idea precisely because they require confidence in your ability to deliver consistent volume.

5. Revenue share

A percentage of the buyer's revenue from converted leads, paid to the lead provider. Conceptually attractive: it aligns incentives, lets buyers "pay only for results," and lets brokers participate in the upside of strong intake. In practice, revenue share is rare in the most lucrative verticals, and the reasons are legal.

Why revenue share is rare and legally tricky

• Legal services. ABA Model Rule 5.4 prohibits fee-sharing with non-lawyers (with narrow exceptions). The majority of state bars treat percentage-of-recovery referral fees paid to non-attorney lead providers as prohibited fee-splitting. ABA Model Rules 7.2 and 7.3 govern lawyer advertising and the use of intermediaries; states vary significantly in how strictly they apply these. Per-lead flat fees structured as advertising payments — not as a percentage of recovery — are the model that survives most state bar scrutiny.

• Insurance. State anti-rebating laws and the NAIC model commission-splitting rules restrict percentage compensation to unlicensed entities. Lead providers without insurance licenses cannot lawfully take a percentage of a carrier's premium or an agent's commission in most states.

• Mortgage and consumer finance. RESPA Section 8 prohibits kickbacks and unearned fees on federally related mortgage loans. The CFPB has been clear in supervisory guidance that pay-per-lead structures are not categorically prohibited but must reflect the reasonable value of services actually rendered. Percentage-of-loan-amount compensation to a lead provider is far harder to defend under Section 8 than per-lead flat fees structured as marketing payments.

• Healthcare and addiction treatment. The federal Anti-Kickback Statute and the 2018 Eliminating Kickbacks in Recovery Act (EKRA) reach broadly. EKRA in particular has been read to prohibit percentage-based compensation to marketers for referrals to recovery and clinical lab services. This is covered in detail in Module 2.

Where revenue share does work: B2B verticals not regulated by professional licensing schemes (some SaaS, some commercial services), and arrangements among already-licensed parties where the regulatory framework permits commission splitting. As a default, assume revenue share is the harder model, not the easier one — even though it intuitively feels simpler.

Why pricing varies — the three drivers

Driver 1: LTV per closed customer

The lead's ceiling price is set by what the buyer can afford to pay and remain profitable. A buyer whose closed-customer lifetime gross margin is $20,000 has more headroom than a buyer whose closed-customer margin is $300. This is why personal injury leads trade at $500+ exclusive and consumer-loan leads trade at $30–60 — the LTV difference between the two customers is multiple orders of magnitude.

Driver 2: Conversion rate from lead to customer

Across two verticals with similar LTV, the one with a better lead-to-customer conversion rate will sustain higher CPL. A 20% conversion rate on $200 leads is the same CAC as a 5% conversion rate on $50 leads — but the variance is lower at the higher conversion rate, which most buyers prefer.

Driver 3: Urgency and decay

Consumer shopping windows differ. Auto insurance shoppers convert within hours; the lead's value decays rapidly. Remodeling shoppers can sit on the decision for months; leads remain workable longer. Higher urgency typically means higher exclusive pricing (the rapid decay punishes shared models more harshly) and a higher premium on speed-to-call.

Choosing a model

A new operator's defaults, in the author's reading of the industry:

  1. Start with shared per-lead flat fee. It's the model with the most precedent, the clearest contractual norms, and the lowest risk for both sides.
  2. Layer in exclusive per-lead when the relationship is established and the buyer wants the higher-converting product.
  3. Consider pay-per-call only if you have or are building a call-routing operation; it's a different operational profile, not just a different price card.
  4. Avoid CPA / pay-per-appointment until your intake-loop understanding of the buyer is strong. CPA pricing rewards buyers with strong intake and punishes those without.
  5. Avoid retainers until you have stable, predictable supply. The cash-flow upside is real; the cash-flow risk if you miss a month is also real.
  6. Avoid revenue share unless you've consulted vertical-specific counsel and have a clear path to compliant structuring.

Hypothesis (clearly marked): the author's read of operator commentary is that the simplest model is also the easiest to scale early. Operators who started with exclusive form leads in one sub-vertical and one geography have an easier time on average than operators who started with three pricing variants and an aggressive CPA play in their first six months. This is a hypothesis, not a tested claim — your situation may differ.

On-camera summary (script anchor for this lesson)

There are five business models. Per-lead flat fee, with exclusive and shared variants, is by far the most common. Pay-per-call is its own thing operationally. CPA — pay per appointment or signed case — is a high-trust model with big upside and real downside. Retainers are agency-flavored. Revenue share is rare and legally fraught in the most lucrative verticals.

Pricing varies more than newcomers expect — personal injury exclusive leads at $250–650+, auto insurance shared at $5–20, Medicare during AEP at $25–60 per qualified call, B2B SaaS qualified leads at $150–500. The variance is driven by LTV, conversion rate, and urgency. Memorize the ranges in your target vertical; recite them when buyers test you.

If you're starting out, default to shared per-lead form leads in one vertical, one geography. Add complexity only when the basics are running.

Lesson 1.4 — Brief industry history

Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you can narrate, in rough order, the major arc of the lead brokering industry from its early-2000s origins to mid-2026; describe the FCC one-to-one consent rule and its 2025 vacating in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC; and articulate why regulatory volatility is intrinsic to this business.

Why history matters in this business

Lead brokering looks like a young industry from the outside, but the playbook in active use today was largely written in 2003–2010 by insurance and mortgage lead generators. The verticals expanded, the platforms got better, and the regulatory landscape moved underneath the business in ways operators are still adjusting to. Reading the arc lets you understand why the industry is structured the way it is — and why it changes so often.

The lesson covers the arc in four passes: origins (2000–2007), expansion (2008–2018), modernization and platform consolidation (2018–2023), and the regulatory crucible of 2023–2026.

Origins: 2000–2007

The first commercial-scale lead brokering operations in the United States emerged in the early 2000s in two adjacent verticals: insurance and mortgage.

In insurance, the breakthrough was the realization that consumers shopping for auto insurance online wanted comparison shopping, and that comparison shopping could be productized as a multi-carrier quote form. NetQuote (founded 1989, scaled in the early 2000s) and InsuranceQuotes (later part of QuinStreet) built the template: drive paid search traffic to a quote form, capture the consumer's basic details and PEWC-equivalent consent language, and sell the lead simultaneously to several participating carriers and agents.

In mortgage, LendingTree (founded 1996, IPO 2000) productized the same idea for home loans: "When banks compete, you win" was the catchphrase. LowerMyBills (acquired by Experian in 2005) built a similar funnel for refinance. The business was extraordinarily profitable during the 2003–2006 refi wave.

Two structural choices made in this era shaped the industry:

  1. Shared (multi-sold) leads became the default. The consumer is told they will be contacted by several providers; the providers race to call first. Conversion rates are lower than exclusive but inventory is monetized multiple times.
  2. The "comparison shopping" framing was made the source of the consent disclosure. The consumer is consenting to be contacted by "participating providers," not to be contacted by a single named entity. This framing later became the central legal question of the 2023–2025 FCC one-to-one consent rule debate.

Expansion: 2008–2018

The 2008 financial crisis broke the mortgage lead generation business for several years. Refinance volume collapsed; many operators pivoted or went under. The survivors consolidated, and the survivors' playbook (multi-sold leads, paid search, multi-carrier consent framing) spread into adjacent verticals.

Personal injury law was the second big wave. By the mid-2010s, paid search costs for terms like "car accident lawyer [city]" had climbed into double digits per click, and a generation of specialized PI lead generators — 4LegalLeads, Legal Brand Marketing, ngage, X Social Media, and many smaller operators — built the infrastructure to monetize that intent. Mass tort emerged as its own subindustry around the same time, with named-litigation-specific funnels (3M earplug, Roundup, hernia mesh, Camp Lejeune, talc) running their own purpose-built generation operations.

Home services lead generation, which had been a meaningful but quiet market, accelerated in the same period. HomeAdvisor (now Angi Leads), Modernize, Networx, and a long tail of vertical-specific operators (HomeYou for remodeling, CraftJack for trades) built scale. Solar emerged as a separate sub-vertical with its own pricing dynamic, driven by federal and state incentive policy.

The TCPA picture in this period was already complicated. Three Supreme Court cases and a long line of class actions shaped the legal landscape:

  • Mims v. Arrow Financial Services (2012) — Supreme Court held that federal courts have jurisdiction over TCPA cases, opening the door to federal class actions.
  • Campbell-Ewald Co. v. Gomez (2016) — held that an unaccepted offer to pay full statutory damages did not moot a TCPA class plaintiff's claim.
  • Facebook v. Duguid (2021) — narrowed the statutory definition of "automatic telephone dialing system" (ATDS). Many predicted reduced TCPA exposure as a result; in practice, plaintiffs shifted to other theories (prerecorded voice claims, DNC violations, and consent-record attacks).

Modernization and platform consolidation: 2018–2023

Two developments defined this period.

First, the consent capture vendor stack matured. ActiveProspect's TrustedForm and Verisk's Jornaya (the latter via the LeadiD acquisition) productized tamper-evident consent records that buyers could require contractually. By the late 2010s, certified consent had moved from a competitive differentiator to table stakes for any meaningful buyer relationship in insurance, mortgage, and (increasingly) legal.

Second, the lead distribution platform layer consolidated. ActiveProspect's LeadConduit became the dominant platform; Boberdoo, Phonexa, and Leadspedia each held meaningful share in specific verticals or operator profiles. The infrastructure stack a new operator could buy off the shelf in 2022 was an order of magnitude better than what existed in 2012.

The Federal Trade Commission was also active in this period. The June 2023 revisions to the FTC Endorsement Guides clarified rules around endorsements, material connections, and what counts as an endorsement (including by AI). The Eliminating Kickbacks in Recovery Act (EKRA) had passed in 2018, reshaping marketing compensation in addiction treatment and clinical laboratory services.

The regulatory crucible: 2023–2026

The four years from late 2023 through May 2026 — the present — have been the most regulatorily active in the industry's history. Three rules and one court decision define the period.

FCC one-to-one consent rule (adopted Dec 2023, vacated Jan 2025)

In December 2023, the FCC adopted a rule amending its interpretation of PEWC under the TCPA. The key provisions:

  • PEWC must be specific to a single seller, named in the consent disclosure.
  • Multi-seller "participating providers" framings would no longer satisfy the PEWC standard for autodialed or prerecorded calls and texts.
  • Consent must be "logically and topically related" to the website where it was captured.

The rule was scheduled to take effect January 27, 2025. The Insurance Marketing Coalition (IMC), a trade group of lead generation and insurance industry operators, filed a petition for review with the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit in March 2024, arguing that the FCC had exceeded its statutory authority — that the TCPA, as written by Congress, did not give the agency the power to redefine PEWC in this way.

On January 24, 2025 — three days before the rule's effective date — the Eleventh Circuit issued its opinion in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC and vacated the one-to-one consent provisions, ruling that the FCC had indeed exceeded its statutory authority. The court was specific: the underlying TCPA standard still requires that consent be "clear and conspicuous" and that the consumer authorize calls or texts using an autodialer or prerecorded voice, but Congress did not authorize the FCC to add the one-to-one limitation.

In September 2025, the FCC formally announced it would not pursue the rule further. As of May 2026, PEWC reverts to the pre-2023 standard — but, importantly, many sophisticated buyers continue to demand one-to-one consent contractually as a defensive posture against future plaintiff-bar attacks. The vacating of the rule did not abolish one-to-one consent as a practice; it abolished it as a regulatory requirement.

FCC revocation rule (effective April 11, 2025)

Separately, the FCC adopted a revocation rule that — unlike one-to-one consent — survived legal challenge and is in force as of May 2026. The key provisions:

  • Consumers may revoke consent through "any reasonable means." This includes a verbal request during a call, a reply text of "STOP" or any variant a reasonable interpretation of which signals revocation, an email, or a written letter.
  • Companies must honor revocation within 10 business days.
  • Revocation applies to all future calls and texts from the seller (and from any party making calls on the seller's behalf), not only to the specific channel through which revocation was made.
  • A single confirmation text within 5 minutes is permitted, provided it contains no marketing content.

FTC Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (effective October 21, 2024)

Adopted by the FTC and effective October 21, 2024. The rule explicitly prohibits fake reviews (including AI-generated fake reviews), buying or selling reviews, insider reviews without disclosure, suppression of negative reviews, and fake social-media indicators (followers, likes). Civil penalties run up to approximately $51,744 per violation.

Although the rule is not lead-broker-specific, it reshapes the content and marketing infrastructure many lead generators rely on. Lead-gen landing pages that use review-style content ("Why customers choose us"), advertiser-facing sales pages, and the operator's own website are all in scope.

CFPB-related action and state attorneys general

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) has been active throughout this period in financial services lead generation — most notably in mortgage (RESPA Section 8 enforcement) and consumer credit (UDAAP claims for misleading lead-gen landing pages). State attorneys general have brought parallel actions under state UDAP (unfair and deceptive acts and practices) statutes, particularly in California, New York, and Massachusetts. State privacy laws — California's CCPA/CPRA and the 18+ state laws that have followed it — now also reach lead generation's sale-of-personal-information practices.

What history is teaching

Three durable lessons from the arc:

  1. Regulatory volatility is intrinsic. Multiple federal agencies, every state attorney general, the state insurance and mortgage regulators, the state bars, and an active plaintiffs' bar all touch this industry. New rules and new enforcement priorities appear every year. Operators who do not maintain a compliance function will be surprised; operators who do will not.
  2. The consent record is the single most important asset on the supply side. Every regulatory shift of the last decade has either tightened the consent standard or shifted the burden of proving consent onto the seller. TrustedForm, Jornaya, and equivalents are not optional in any vertical where serious buyers operate.
  3. Verticals can change shape quickly. Solar lost 50%+ of its meaningful operators in 2023–2024 from a combination of net-metering policy changes (California's NEM 3.0 in particular) and supply-side overheating. Mass tort can boom and bust on litigation timing. Any business plan that assumes the vertical you start in will look the same in five years is making an assumption history doesn't support.

On-camera summary (script anchor for this lesson)

The industry was built in insurance and mortgage in the 2000s, expanded into legal and home services in the 2010s, and ran headfirst into a wave of federal regulation between 2023 and 2026. The biggest single event of the period was the FCC's one-to-one consent rule — adopted in December 2023, scheduled to take effect on January 27, 2025, and vacated by the Eleventh Circuit three days earlier in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC. The FCC formally walked away from the rule in September 2025.

Two other rules from the same period are still in force as of May 2026: the FCC's revocation rule (effective April 11, 2025) and the FTC's consumer reviews and testimonials rule (effective October 21, 2024). Both matter; Module 3 covers both in detail.

The history-level takeaway is simple. Multiple agencies regulate this industry, the rules change frequently, and the consent record is the single most valuable asset on the supply side. Build like that's true, because it is.

Lesson 1.5 — Why the industry exists

Learning objective

By the end of this lesson, you can articulate, in CAC/LTV terms, why service businesses outsource lead acquisition rather than running it in-house. You can do a back-of-envelope unit-economics calculation for a representative buyer in a vertical of your choice. You can also list at least three scenarios in which in-house generation makes more economic sense than buying leads.

The economics in one paragraph

Every service business that buys leads has a customer acquisition cost (CAC) ceiling. Above that ceiling, the cost of acquiring a customer eats more of the customer's gross profit than the business can sustainably bear. Below the ceiling, the buyer's marketing investment is profitable. A lead broker exists in the spread between the broker's per-lead cost and the buyer's per-customer CAC ceiling — and the spread exists, in turn, because performance marketing is a specialized skill that not every service business can execute well in-house.

That paragraph is the whole industry. The rest of this lesson unpacks why it's true, what the numbers look like, and when the logic flips.

CAC, LTV, and the headroom math

Three numbers, in dollars per acquired customer:

  • LTV (lifetime value): total gross profit the customer will generate over their relationship with the business.
  • CAC (customer acquisition cost): total marketing and sales cost to acquire one customer.
  • Payback period: months until the cumulative gross profit from the customer covers the CAC.

The headroom for a service business is the LTV minus the CAC. A business with $10,000 LTV and $1,000 CAC has $9,000 of headroom per customer; a business with $1,000 LTV and $800 CAC has $200 of headroom and a much smaller margin for error.

The CAC itself is built from a chain: cost per lead × leads per closed customer = CAC.

At those numbers, paying $200 per lead and converting 1 in 10 produces a $2,000 CAC against a $15,000–$20,000 net case fee — a healthy margin. The buyer can rationally pay considerably more for leads, up to the point where their CAC consumes their net case fee minus their internal costs of carrying the case.

Now flip a single variable. If intake conversion drops from 10% to 5% — the same lead, the same buyer, but a weaker intake operation — CAC doubles to $4,000. The headroom per case is now $11,000–$16,000. Still profitable, but less so. Drop intake further to 3% and CAC climbs to $6,667; the headroom is $8,000–$13,000 and the buyer is starting to feel the squeeze. Drop the conversion rate to 2% and CAC hits $10,000 — at which point the buyer's economics on this lead source are no longer attractive.

The reading: the broker's price is a smaller driver of buyer economics than the buyer's intake conversion rate. A $200 lead with a 10% close is the same CAC as a $400 lead with a 20% close. This is why operator conversations about leads sound like fights about quality, not price. Quality is the term that controls conversion, and conversion controls CAC.

The specialization theorem

If service businesses can run marketing in-house, why don't they? Sometimes they do. But most do not, and the reasons hold up to scrutiny:

  1. Performance marketing is a high-skill activity. A competent Meta Ads or Google Ads media buyer earns between $80,000 and $160,000 a year, with senior practitioners higher. Tools, experimentation budget, and creative production add to the total cost. A solo attorney spending $15,000–$50,000 a year on leads cannot rationally hire a $100,000+ marketer to capture them in-house.
  2. Scale economics matter. A specialist running campaigns across many buyers in a vertical learns faster, gets better data signals, negotiates better with platforms, and amortizes creative work across a larger base. The single buyer cannot match this learning rate.
  3. Capital and risk. Paid marketing requires front-loaded ad spend. A lead broker carries the spend risk between when an ad runs and when a buyer is invoiced. A solo buyer carrying that risk on their own balance sheet is taking a meaningful concentration risk on a single channel.
  4. Compliance burden. Consent capture infrastructure, DNC scrubbing, certified consent records, vendor relationships with TrustedForm and equivalents — these have a fixed-cost component that's painful to absorb at small scale and routine at the scale of a serious lead generator or aggregator.

Each of these reasons creates a per-buyer economic case for outsourcing. Together, they explain why a substantial industry exists between consumers and service providers — even when the service providers could, technically, run their own marketing.

When in-house wins

The economic case for outsourcing flips in identifiable scenarios:

  • Very high LTV. Mass-tort plaintiff firms, enterprise SaaS, ultra-high-net-worth wealth management — the per-customer profit is large enough that internalizing the marketing function is rational.
  • Strong existing brand or organic channel. A buyer that ranks #1 organically for the high-intent terms in their vertical has a CAC structure that bought leads cannot beat.
  • Strict, non-delegable compliance posture. HIPAA-covered entities handling protected health information sometimes cannot delegate consent-capture and marketing functions in a way that survives a HIPAA audit. The economics force these operators in-house.
  • Adversarial relationship with available lead providers. In small or specialty verticals, sometimes the available brokers' inventory is so consistently poor for the buyer's intake that the in-house option is cheaper despite the disadvantages above.
  • Geographic monopoly or near-monopoly. A buyer who is the only credible provider in their geography may have so much consumer demand routed directly to them via brand search and referrals that buying leads becomes incremental rather than primary.

The buyer's tolerance for chain length

Recall from Lesson 1.2 that the lead chain often has multiple intermediaries — traffic source, generator, network, broker, advertiser. Each layer takes a margin. The buyer's tolerance for chain length is set by their CAC math: they can afford a long chain only if their LTV-and-conversion math leaves room for everyone in it to be paid.

When a buyer's CAC math gets tight (because the vertical is competing harder, because conversion drops, because LTV erodes), the buyer's first move is almost always to shorten the chain. They go direct to the network or to the generator, cutting out the broker. Or they bring some portion of generation in-house. This is why the broker layer is the most vulnerable in any chain that comes under economic pressure — and why the durable brokers are the ones who add value the network can't easily replicate (vertical specialization, intake training, geographic depth, relationship continuity).

What the buyer is actually paying for

From the buyer's perspective, the per-lead price covers, in some mix:

  1. Compliant traffic. The generator paid for the consumer's attention through compliant channels and captured a defensible consent record.
  2. Filtering. The lead matches the buyer's contracted criteria — geography, qualification, demographic, timing.
  3. Speed. The lead arrives in the buyer's CRM in time to be worked while the consumer is still actively shopping.
  4. Selection. The buyer didn't have to evaluate dozens of generators, sign as many contracts, or manage the supply directly.
  5. Risk absorption. The broker carries the spend risk and (under most contracts) some portion of the lead-quality risk.

When a buyer says "your leads are expensive," they almost always mean one of these five components feels under-delivered. The diagnostic conversation with a buyer is rarely about price in the abstract; it's about which of the five jobs above the leads are or aren't doing.

On-camera summary (script anchor for this lesson)

Lead brokering exists because the marketing job that has to happen between consumers and service providers is a specialized job that most service providers can't run efficiently in-house. The broker captures the spread between what they pay for traffic and what the buyer is willing to pay per lead, and the buyer is willing to pay that because their CAC math has headroom that a specialist can produce more reliably than they can.

The single most important number in this industry is the buyer's intake conversion rate, not the broker's lead price. A $200 lead that converts at 10% is the same CAC as a $400 lead that converts at 20%. When buyers complain about price, they almost always mean quality.

The industry's economic structure isn't accidental. It's built on the specialization theorem, the scale economics of paid media, and the buyer's tolerance for chain length set by their LTV math. Read every operator conversation through that lens and most of what's confusing about the industry becomes legible.
