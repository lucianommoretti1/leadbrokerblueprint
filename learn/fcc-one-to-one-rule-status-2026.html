FCC one-to-one consent rule status in 2026 — what lead generators need to know today

The FCC's "one-to-one consent" rule has been one of the most consequential regulatory developments in lead generation in a decade. Here's where it actually stands in 2026, what the 11th Circuit's January 2025 decision changed, and what compliant operators are doing today.

Not legal advice. Educational summary only. Consult counsel for your specific situation.

The short version

The FCC's one-to-one consent rule was vacated by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit on January 24, 2025 in Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC. The court held that the FCC had exceeded its statutory authority under the TCPA. As of mid-2026, the rule is NOT in force at the federal level. The pre-existing TCPA framework continues to apply.

Bottom line for operators: consent disclosures may still name multiple sellers in a single flow, provided the consumer can clearly identify who may contact them. The FCC has not, as of mid-2026, issued a replacement rule. State-level activity continues — see below.

Background — what the rule was supposed to do

In December 2023, the FCC adopted the one-to-one consent rule as part of broader changes to TCPA implementation regulations. The core idea: end the practice of "lead-gen consent farms" where consumers checking a single box on a comparison-shopping site simultaneously consented to telemarketing calls from dozens or hundreds of "marketing partners." The rule required, in effect, that Prior Express Written Consent (PEWC) be granted for each specific seller individually.

The rule was scheduled to take effect on January 27, 2025. It had been delayed several times due to industry pushback and concerns about implementation complexity. Compliance-focused operators in the lead-gen industry had largely been preparing for the rule for over a year.

The 11th Circuit's decision

On January 24, 2025 — three days before the rule was set to take effect — the Eleventh Circuit issued its decision in Insurance Marketing Coalition v. FCC. The court held that the FCC had exceeded the statutory authority granted to it under the TCPA. The TCPA, the court reasoned, sets out the standards Congress chose for "prior express written consent" and does not authorize the FCC to impose additional, more restrictive requirements.

The decision vacated the rule entirely. It was not stayed, narrowed, or remanded — it was struck down.

What this means in practice (as of mid-2026)

  1. The pre-existing PEWC framework applies. Consent disclosures may name multiple sellers and may include linked "marketing partner" lists, provided the disclosure is clear and conspicuous and the consumer can identify who may contact them.
  2. The TCPA itself is unchanged. Statutory damages of $500 per violation (treble for willful/knowing) remain. The underlying requirement that consent be valid for the actual seller making contact remains.
  3. State-level activity is escalating. Several states have proposed or enacted their own one-to-one-style rules at the state level. Operators must comply with both federal and state law.
  4. Best-in-class operators are voluntarily continuing one-to-one-style consent flows. The view: more defensible in litigation, less vulnerable to future rule changes, and aligned with the direction the industry is generally heading.

What's likely to happen next

The FCC may attempt to re-issue some version of the rule with revised statutory grounding. Congress may amend the TCPA itself. Either could happen — neither is certain. Operators should:

  • Stay current via direct monitoring of FCC dockets (CG Docket 02-278 and successors).
  • Monitor state-level activity in CA, FL, NY, TX, IL, WA — the most active state-regulatory environments for lead generation.
  • Maintain documentation of all consent capture in 4+ year retention windows.
  • Treat one-to-one-style consent flows as the strategic default even when not legally required.
Watch for: Any reinstatement attempt by the FCC under new statutory framing, state legislation passing one-to-one rules at the state level (CA AB 1138 was a notable 2025 attempt), and the FCC's pending revocation rule enforcement priorities.
